## The official record is not the whole working memory

An evidence management system can tell you where an item is, who touched it, and what the official custody history says.

It may not tell you why a release is still sitting on your desk, which supervisor promised an answer, whether a destruction batch is waiting on one missing approval, or what odd discrepancy needs to be checked before anything moves.

That is the layer this pad is trying to preserve.

## The pad cannot become a second system of record

That boundary matters.

A completed sheet must not become the only place an official hold, transfer, release decision, or custody event exists. The evidence system and agency procedures remain authoritative.

The pad is closer to a continuity note for the custodian: what is unfinished, what is blocking it, and what should happen next.

## A deliberately small set of prompts

The first structure is:

- **Reference**
- **Current state**
- **Action pending**
- **Waiting on**
- **Next check**
- **Do not release or move until**
- **Critical context**

The last two prompts need testing. If they encourage users to rely on the paper instead of the official system, they should change.

## Why 5 x 7

A 4 x 6 card works for the compact state of one forensic examination. Evidence-room tasks often include names, dates, multiple items, and a little more administrative context.

Five by seven is a working hypothesis: still small enough to live beside a keyboard, but large enough for normal handwriting.

## The next honest gate

The next pass should load the pad with representative scenarios: pending property release, transfer to another agency, inventory discrepancy, destruction batch, court hold follow-up, and vendor/service issue.

If the same fields survive all six without becoming vague, the structure earns a printable proof.
