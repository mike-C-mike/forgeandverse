## The evidence room has projects too

Property and evidence work is often described item by item: receive, store, transfer, release, dispose.

The room itself has a second layer of work that is much less tidy.

An inventory takes weeks. A destruction project crosses multiple approvals. A discrepancy needs research. A freezer alarm leads to service calls and temporary storage decisions. A shelving project affects hundreds of locations. An audit produces questions that do not resolve in one afternoon.

Those are projects, even if nobody calls them that.

## A notebook for the room, not the evidence

The working ledger is deliberately named for the room rather than for individual items.

It should help a custodian capture:

- what project or issue is active;
- what has already been checked;
- who was contacted;
- what decision was made;
- what remains unresolved;
- when the next checkpoint should happen.

It should not contain the authoritative custody history for an item.

## Mostly blank still wins

The Examiner's Notebook taught the studio an important rule: professional notebooks become less useful when the designer becomes too impressed with the form.

The same rule applies here.

A few front pages may orient projects, decisions, contacts, and open issues. Most of the interior should remain ordinary writing space that can handle lists, diagrams, shelf maps, calculations, meeting notes, or whatever the room actually demands.

## The phrase Working Ledger needs to earn itself

Ledger carries useful weight: continuity, memory, accountability.

It also risks sounding official.

That tension is part of the design test. The cover and interior need to make the book feel serious without suggesting that it replaces the agency evidence system, required logs, or formal records.

If that distinction cannot be made cleanly, the name should change before production.

## The next honest gate

The next pass should prototype two trim sizes and a thirty-two-page interior dummy with no more than five structured pages.

The question is not whether we can invent more fields. It is whether the book gives the custodian a better place to think.
