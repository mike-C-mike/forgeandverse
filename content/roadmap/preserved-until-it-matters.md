## What the first proof got wrong

The original teddy-bear composition came from a meaningful truth: an object can sit quietly in evidence for years before a later fact gives it extraordinary significance.

As a story, that works.

As the central visual for Forge & Verse, the bear started doing too much. It made the piece feel like it belonged to one anecdote instead of to the people who preserve thousands of ordinary objects without knowing which one will matter later. It also pulled the work toward sentimentality when the stronger subject is discipline.

The v20 direction retires the bear as the hero.

## Custody over time becomes visible

The new visual grammar uses things an evidence professional actually lives with:

- shelf geometry
- generic labels
- seals
- repeated identifiers without real case information
- one ordinary, anonymous retained item
- a long horizontal rhythm that can suggest elapsed time without drawing a literal timeline

Those details are not there to make the piece look "forensic." They are evidence that someone received, identified, stored, and preserved the item carefully enough for a future question to remain possible.

## The central thought stays

**Preservation gives truth time to catch up.**

That line survives because it describes the value of the work without requiring a dramatic object. The title carries the long horizon. The visual should make that patience feel physical.

## What the next composition must avoid

The next art proof should not use a generic crime-scene aesthetic, glowing evidence tags, yellow tape, weapon silhouettes, fingerprint overlays, or invented case details. It should also avoid borrowing the specifics of the story that originally inspired the idea.

The goal is recognition, not costume.

## The next honest gate

The direction is stronger, but the art is not finished. The next round needs one full composition built from this custody-centered grammar and then judged on paper and low-sheen metal at realistic wall scale.
