A digital forensics request often arrives disguised as a technical instruction.

> Extract the phone.
>
> Pull everything.
>
> Run the device through the tool.

Those statements describe an action. They do not describe the question the examination is meant to help answer.

That distinction matters because a modern device can contain years of communications, location records, photographs, account activity, application data, documents, browsing history, and artifacts that only become meaningful when they are connected to a specific event or allegation. The device may be full of information and still provide no direction.

An examiner cannot responsibly treat every possible artifact as equally relevant. Authority creates boundaries. Scope defines what may be examined. The investigative objective explains why the permitted data matters.

## Begin with what must be resolved

A useful request tells the examiner what the investigation is trying to establish, locate, confirm, or exclude.

For example:

- Identify communications between named parties during a defined date range.
- Determine whether the device was used to access a particular account.
- Locate images or location information relevant to a specified incident.
- Identify records showing whether a named application was used during the period under investigation.

Those objectives do not guarantee that the evidence exists or that a particular tool will recover it. They give the examination a defensible direction.

The difference is not semantic decoration. It changes how an examiner approaches the work.

A request for “everything” pushes the burden of relevance onto the forensic unit. A clear objective allows the examiner to connect authorized sources, appropriate methods, search terms, date ranges, and reporting decisions to the investigative need.

## Context is not contamination

Investigators sometimes hesitate to provide context because they do not want to bias the examiner. That concern is understandable, but an examiner does not need to be kept ignorant in order to remain objective.

The examiner needs enough context to understand aliases, relationships, expected activity, relevant applications, important dates, possible remote-wipe risk, and facts that may change how the evidence should be preserved or interpreted.

Context should not dictate the conclusion. It should make the question intelligible.

The examiner still has to separate what the evidence shows from what someone expected it to show. That is part of the discipline.

## The tool is not the objective

Requesters may know the name of a forensic platform because it is the visible part of the process. They may ask for Cellebrite, Magnet AXIOM, FTK, or another familiar tool as shorthand for the result they want.

The forensic unit should translate that shorthand back into the actual need.

A tool is a method. It may be appropriate, partially appropriate, unavailable, unnecessary, or incapable of producing the requested data. The investigative objective should survive even when the chosen method changes.

That is why “run the phone through Cellebrite” is weaker than “identify communications between these parties from June 1 through June 14.” The second statement remains useful regardless of which acquisition or analysis path the examiner determines is appropriate.

## A better handoff protects both sides

A clear intake helps the requester because it reduces delays and repeated questions. It helps the examiner because authority, scope, targets, evidence condition, and urgency are visible before work begins. It also creates a better record of why the examination was requested and what it was intended to address.

The intake should not become a forty-page obstacle. It only needs to capture the information someone will actually use to route, preserve, prioritize, examine, document, or accept the request.

The most important field may be the simplest:

> What question should this examination help answer?

A device is evidence. It is not the question.
