When I was younger, I loved the *Uncle John’s Bathroom Reader* books.

They were perfect for a brain that wanted to know a little bit about almost everything. History, science, strange facts, language, trivia, stories, things that were useful, things that were completely useless, and things that somehow became permanent residents in my head even though I have not thought about the book they came from in decades.

The normal way to read a book like that would probably be to open it somewhere in the middle, read whatever caught your attention, close it, and come back later.

I did not do that.

I started at the beginning and read them linearly, cover to cover.

Of course I did.

I can look back now and see something very familiar in that behavior. Curiosity is one of the traits I value most in myself. I love learning. I like disappearing into subjects I knew almost nothing about an hour earlier. I like finding connections between ideas that arrived from completely different places. A ridiculous amount of knowledge has accumulated in my head because I have spent most of my life following whatever caught my attention next.

That lack of focus gave me something valuable. I have a broad foundation that lets me recognize patterns across fields, pull an idea out of an unexpected place, or remember some obscure fact years after it should have evaporated.

It also contains a warning.

A strength can become a weakness without ever changing its name.

Curiosity can become distraction. Persistence can become stubbornness. Confidence can become arrogance. Patience can become passivity. Loyalty can become blindness. Protectiveness can become control. Discipline can become rigidity. Ambition can become an inability to enjoy anything already achieved.

Even intentionality, the thing I am trying to bring more deliberately into my life through these essays, can become another trap.

That is the problem I want to examine here.

The first essay in this series was about steering. I spent a large portion of my life moving without thinking very deliberately about direction. *The Horizon* is, in part, my attempt to change that. I want to identify questions worth asking, choose areas of my life worth examining, read deeply instead of merely collecting impressions, and put enough structure around the process that I do not simply careen from interesting idea to interesting idea forever.

But there is an obvious danger in responding to drift by worshipping the plan.

I know myself well enough to see it.

I can decide I want to be intentional, create a reading list, organize the reading list, build a research system, define a sequence, establish a framework, optimize the framework, create categories for the framework, and eventually spend so much time constructing the perfect system for learning that the system becomes the thing I am actually doing.

The project meant to create freedom becomes another set of rails.

That is where one of the oldest ideas in moral philosophy started becoming far more interesting to me than the slogan version I had absorbed over the years.

## The middle is not mediocrity

“Everything in moderation” is easy to say and easy to misunderstand.

It can sound like an argument for blandness. Never care too much. Never commit too deeply. Never push too hard. Stay safely in the middle of everything and call that wisdom.

That is not especially compelling to me, and it is not what Aristotle was arguing when he described moral virtue as a mean between excess and deficiency.

In Book II of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle describes character as something shaped through practice and habit. We become just by doing just things, courageous by acting courageously, temperate by practicing temperance. Virtue is not merely an opinion we hold about ourselves. It is something trained into character through repeated choices.[^1]

His idea of the “mean” is also much more demanding than simply splitting the difference.

Courage is not fifty percent cowardice and fifty percent recklessness. Generosity is not an arithmetic midpoint between giving nothing and giving away everything you own. The appropriate response depends on the person, the situation, the reason, the timing, and the thing being done. Aristotle explicitly distinguishes the mean “relative to us” from a mathematical average. What is too much for one person may be too little for another.[^1]

That matters because it changes moderation from a quantity problem into a judgment problem.

The question is not, “How much courage should a person have?”

The question is, “What does courage require here?”

Sometimes courage means stepping forward. Sometimes it means backing away from a fight your ego desperately wants. Sometimes patience means waiting. Sometimes patience has already lasted too long and action is overdue. Sometimes honesty requires saying something uncomfortable. Sometimes the honest thing does not need to be delivered with maximum force simply because maximum force feels more pure.

The middle moves because life moves.

That makes balance much harder than choosing an extreme. Extremes can be simple. Rules can be simple. “Always” and “never” are wonderfully efficient words because they remove most of the burden of judgment.

Virtue often puts the burden back.

Aristotle goes even further. Not everything has a virtuous midpoint. He specifically argues that some acts are wrong in themselves rather than becoming acceptable when performed in the correct amount.[^1] Moderation cannot be used as a universal excuse for moral fence-sitting. There is no obligation to find the reasonable amount of cruelty, corruption, or betrayal.

That distinction is important because “moderation” can become its own failure mode.

A person can be so committed to balance that they refuse to take a stand when a stand is required. They can confuse nuance with indecision, compromise with wisdom, and avoiding conflict with being reasonable.

So when I say I am interested in moderation, I do not mean I want less conviction.

I mean I want better proportion.

## Every virtue casts a shadow

I have started thinking about desirable traits as having at least three positions: deficiency, virtue, and excess.

The exact labels will change depending on the trait, and real human behavior is obviously messier than a three-column chart. But the framework gives me a useful question whenever I identify something I want more of in myself.

What happens if I get too good at this?

That question is surprisingly effective because self-improvement usually points in only one direction.

More discipline.

More confidence.

More productivity.

More resilience.

More empathy.

More patience.

More ambition.

More focus.

Those all sound good because we usually encounter them while imagining their absence. A person struggling with procrastination wants discipline. A person afraid to speak wants confidence. A person who gives up too quickly wants persistence.

But once the trait has been developed, continuing in the same direction does not necessarily continue producing virtue.

Discipline can rescue someone from drift and later imprison them in routines they no longer need. Confidence can allow someone to trust their judgment and later make them stop listening. Persistence can carry someone through difficulty and later keep them committed to a decision that new evidence should have killed months ago.

The same mechanism that fixes one problem can create the next one.

That does not mean the original lesson was wrong. It means context changed.

This is one reason I resist the idea that personal development should end with finding the correct system and following it forever. A tool can be exactly what I need in one stage of life and become counterproductive in another. The trait that needs strengthening today may need restraint five years from now.

My own curiosity makes a useful example.

I do not want less of it. Curiosity has shaped nearly everything I enjoy about learning. It has helped me move between technology, history, philosophy, psychology, leadership, fiction, comedy, and whatever other subject wandered into view. It is probably part of why a random line from a comedy sketch can sit in my brain for more than twenty years and then suddenly become the closing idea of an essay about personal philosophy.

But curiosity without structure can become endless accumulation.

That is where the Bathroom Readers come back in.

There is something funny about taking books built for casual dipping and treating them like a linear course of study, but there is also something revealing about it. My instinct is not merely to sample something interesting. Once I care, I often want the whole thing. I want to understand how the pieces connect. I want to know what came before and what comes next.

That tendency has given me an enormous amount of strange, durable knowledge.

It can also make stopping difficult.

A person can spend a lifetime gathering information and never pause long enough to ask what any of it is for.

The goal of *The Horizon Essays* is not to cure me of curiosity. It is to give curiosity a little more direction.

That sounds simple until direction starts trying to become control.

## Structure the questions, not the answers

When I first started thinking seriously about this project, it was tempting to build the entire route in advance.

There are philosophers I want to read. Traditions I want to understand. Leadership ideas I want to interrogate. Psychological research I want to compare against older moral claims. Questions about fatherhood, ambition, character, resilience, technology, identity, meaning, and responsibility that I know deserve more attention than I have given them.

Making a list is useful.

Believing the list owns me is not.

The distinction I keep coming back to is this:

**Structure the questions, not the answers.**

I want enough structure to avoid drifting. I do not want so much structure that the research is no longer allowed to surprise me.

If I decide today that Essay Nine must be about one subject, and six months of reading convinces me that the subject is boring, redundant, or based on a premise I no longer accept, continuing to write it would not be discipline. It would be obedience to a dead plan.

If one book sends me into a subject I had not anticipated, following that path is not failure to stick to the program. It may be the program working.

If I decide a thinker I expected to admire has very little I want to keep, I should be allowed to say so.

If a question turns out to need three essays instead of one, that is fine.

If a question turns out not to deserve an essay at all, that is fine too.

The plan is there to make the exploration more intentional. The exploration does not exist to justify the plan.

That sounds obvious when written down, which probably means I will need to remind myself of it repeatedly.

There is a particular kind of satisfaction in completing a list. Checking boxes feels like movement. A reading plan can quietly transform from “these are things worth examining” into “these are things I must finish because Past Me typed them into a document.”

Those are not the same thing.

I am trying to become more deliberate, not more obedient to my own paperwork.

The funny part is that this may be exactly the kind of distinction Aristotle’s framework is good at exposing. Drift sits on one side. Rigidity sits on the other. Somewhere between them is intentionality.

But “between” does not mean equal distance.

There may be seasons when I need considerably more structure because I have been bouncing among distractions. There may be seasons when the healthiest move is to throw out half the plan and follow something alive.

The correct amount is relative to the person and the moment.

That makes the work less clean, but probably more honest.

## The failure mode of self-improvement

The more I think about virtues having excess states, the more suspicious I become of the phrase “best version of yourself” when it is treated carelessly.

I still use it. I still mean it.

The horizon in the title of this series is the best version of myself I can currently describe. I want to move in that direction. I want to be a better father, husband, leader, friend, professional, and human being than I would become through inertia alone.

But self-improvement can become a remarkably sophisticated way to dislike yourself.

There is always another weakness to fix.

Another habit to build.

Another book to read.

Another system to optimize.

Another deficiency to identify.

Another future version of yourself who is supposedly calmer, stronger, wiser, healthier, more productive, more present, better read, more patient, more disciplined, and somehow also sleeping eight hours a night.

The horizon moves. That is the point.

But a person can spend so much time staring at the horizon that they stop noticing where they are standing.

That would be an especially stupid failure for me now.

I have children growing up while I am trying to become a better father. I have a marriage happening while I am trying to become a better husband. I have a life happening while I am researching how to live one.

The improvement cannot consume the thing it is supposed to improve.

That may be the most important application of moderation in this entire series.

The pursuit of a better self has to be moderated by respect for the current one.

Not complacency. Not the belief that I am finished. Not some soothing declaration that everything about me is already exactly as it should be.

Respect.

The person I am today is not raw material whose only value is becoming someone else tomorrow.

He is the one actually living the life.

That creates a tension I do not think can be permanently solved. I want ambition without permanent dissatisfaction. Gratitude without complacency. Discipline without rigidity. Confidence without certainty. Humility without self-erasure. Curiosity without endless distraction. Patience without passivity. Love without possession. Protection without control.

I doubt there is a formula that keeps every one of those perfectly calibrated.

Maybe that is another important lesson hidden inside Aristotle’s mean. Moral judgment cannot be completely outsourced to a formula because the relevant amount changes with circumstances. The person has to develop the judgment capable of recognizing what the situation requires.

That is harder than memorizing rules.

It is also more interesting.

## Balance is active

The word “balance” sometimes creates an image of stillness, as though the goal is to discover one perfect position and remain there.

That does not fit my experience.

Balance is active.

Anyone who has ever carried something awkward, ridden a bicycle, stood on a boat, or tried not to fall while a three-year-old unexpectedly changes direction beneath their feet understands that balance involves constant small corrections.

You do not achieve it once.

You respond.

That may be a better way for me to think about character as well.

There probably is not a single permanent setting called “appropriate confidence.” My confidence should change depending on the subject. There are areas where experience gives me good reason to trust my judgment and areas where the responsible position is to know I am a beginner.

There is no universal setting for patience. Sometimes another person deserves time. Sometimes waiting becomes avoidance.

There is no universal setting for persistence. Sometimes quitting is weakness. Sometimes quitting is evidence that you finally listened to reality.

This is why I like the idea that every desired trait has a failure mode.

It forces me to look in both directions.

If I only ask, “How do I become more disciplined?” I have already assumed that more discipline is the answer.

A better set of questions is: Where am I undisciplined? Where has discipline helped me? Where has it become rigid? What would the right amount look like in this situation?

That is slower.

It is less satisfying than a commandment.

It is also much harder to turn into a motivational poster.

But it may produce a better life.

I can imagine eventually building something like a personal virtue ledger, not as a fixed scorecard, but as a way of checking the traits I say I value against their shadows. Courage can fail through cowardice or recklessness. Confidence can fail through timidity or arrogance. Curiosity can fail through indifference or obsession. Planning can fail through carelessness or overcontrol. Patience can fail through impulsiveness or passivity.

The point would not be to locate myself exactly in the middle of every line.

The point would be to notice which direction I am leaning.

Aristotle makes a similar observation in Book II when he suggests that people should pay attention to the extreme toward which they are personally most prone and deliberately correct for it.[^1] That feels less like an ancient abstraction and more like practical advice.

My correction will not always be the same as someone else’s.

A person who never plans may need a calendar.

A person who cannot deviate from the calendar may need to ignore it for an afternoon.

The tool is not the virtue.

The judgment is.

## Especially your virtues

There is another reason I want this idea near the beginning of the series.

The people and philosophies I am going to study will offer traits worth admiring. Stoicism may sharpen resilience and self-command. Aristotle will have much more to say about character and flourishing. Other traditions will challenge attachment, ambition, ego, duty, individualism, certainty, and even the nature of the self I am supposedly trying to improve.

I expect to find ideas I want to steal.

I also expect that some of the most dangerous versions of those ideas will look almost identical to the virtues that attracted me in the first place.

Resilience can become refusal to ask for help.

Self-command can become emotional suppression.

Acceptance can become passivity.

Duty can become self-neglect.

Independence can become isolation.

Empathy can become an inability to set boundaries.

Conviction can become dogmatism.

Humility can become performative smallness.

That does not make the virtues fake. It makes proportion part of the virtue.

This is where my version of “everything in moderation” is starting to land.

I do not mean that every belief should be diluted until nothing matters very much. I do not mean the correct answer is always halfway between two people arguing. I do not mean intensity is suspicious or that extraordinary effort is inherently unhealthy.

Sometimes the right amount is a lot.

Sometimes the right response is absolute.

Sometimes the moral middle is nowhere near the numerical middle.

What I mean is that the traits I admire do not get a permanent exemption from scrutiny simply because I have labeled them virtues.

If something is genuinely good, I should still be willing to ask what it looks like when distorted by excess.

Especially when I am good at it.

Especially when I am proud of it.

Especially when other people praise me for it.

Those may be the moments when the shadow becomes hardest to see.

The Bathroom Readers are harmless. Reading a trivia book from cover to cover is not exactly a cautionary tale of human excess. But the instinct underneath it is recognizable enough to make me smile.

Give me a subject and I will keep going.

Give me a system and I may try to optimize it.

Give me a plan and there is a decent chance I will start treating completion as evidence that the plan was good.

Those tendencies have served me well often enough that I have no interest in eliminating them.

I want to govern them.

That is what moderation means to me right now.

Not less curiosity. Better-directed curiosity.

Not less discipline. Discipline capable of changing course.

Not less ambition. Ambition that leaves room to enjoy the life it is building.

Not less planning. A plan with permission to become obsolete.

Not less commitment to becoming better. A commitment that does not require treating the present as a waiting room.

The first essay was about realizing I could steer.

This one is about remembering not to lock the wheel.

The Horizon needs direction, but the road still gets to curve.

If this project becomes exactly the project I can already imagine today, then I probably planned it too tightly. I want to fill gaps in my knowledge, interrogate ideas I have carried casually for years, discover where I am wrong, and understand more deeply how any of it should affect the way I actually live.

That requires structure.

It also requires enough freedom for the answer to surprise me.

So I will keep the reading lists. I will keep the questions. I will keep trying to approach this with more intentionality than I brought to much of my earlier life.

I will also cross things out.

I will change the order.

I will follow detours.

I will stop reading books that have stopped earning my attention.

I will abandon questions that turn out to be less interesting than I thought.

I will let a new idea interrupt the plan when it deserves to.

That is not a failure of discipline.

It may be discipline learning proportion.

Everything in moderation.

Especially your virtues.

**Balance is not a compromise of virtue. Balance is part of virtue.**
